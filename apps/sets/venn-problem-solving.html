Two sets first

Use paper, a shared screen or a whiteboard. One partner explains the diagram; the other asks for reasons. Then swap.

Round 1 · Two sets

15 play football, 12 play an instrument, and 5 do both.

  1. Partner A: draw two overlapping ovals F and M. Put 5 in the shared region first.
  2. Partner B: ask why the shared 5 must be placed before the “only” counts.
  3. Together: find football only, music only and at least one activity.
  4. Swap: Partner B explains why 15 + 12 counts the shared students twice.
Reveal one worked solution

Football only is 15 − 5 = 10. Music only is 12 − 5 = 7. At least one is 10 + 5 + 7 = 22. The check is 10 + 5 + 7 = 22.

Round 2 · Three-set stretch

Set totals are A = 20, B = 18 and C = 15.

There are 5 in A and B only, 4 in A and C only, 3 in B and C only, and 2 in all three.

  1. Partner A: draw and label the eight regions.
  2. Partner B: calculate the three “only” regions and explain each subtraction.
  3. Together: find exactly two and at least two.
  4. Swap: explain how adding A + B + C can count a student more than once.
Reveal the three-set solution

A only is 20 − 5 − 4 − 2 = 9. B only is 18 − 5 − 3 − 2 = 8. C only is 15 − 4 − 3 − 2 = 6. Exactly two is 5 + 4 + 3 = 12; at least two is 12 + 2 = 14. The union is 9 + 8 + 6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 37.

Support: give each partner a region-label card: A only · both · B only.

Core: solve the two-set problem and write a sentence for each operation.

Stretch: create new totals and overlap counts, then ask another pair to find the two only regions and the union.

Sets are collections of distinct elements. Diagrams, words and symbols should tell the same story.
