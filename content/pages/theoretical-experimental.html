If we know the numbers of red and blue counters, we can calculate a probability. If the counters are hidden, we can instead draw samples and estimate it.

1. Theoretical probability: when the counters are visible

There are 6 red and 4 blue counters. A counter is chosen at random. What is the probability that it is red?

P(red) = 6/10 = 0.6 = 60%

Predict the result first.

Every counter has an equal chance of being selected.

2. Experimental probability: when the counters are hidden

A well-mixed bag contains 1,000 counters, some red and some blue. We do not know the proportions. Draw a sample of ten counters without replacement, record the results, and repeat. Use the observed relative frequency to estimate P(red).

Important: Drawing without replacement changes the bag after each draw. This experiment models an unknown fixed mixture; the estimate becomes more informative as more of the bag is sampled. It does not assume that draws are independent.

3. Think about it

  1. Can the theoretical probability be calculated in the first experiment? Why?
  2. In the second experiment, can you calculate the exact probability before seeing the bag's composition?
  3. Would drawing 100 counters usually give a more stable estimate than drawing 10?
  4. Does drawing without replacement make the successive draws independent?

Big idea: Theoretical probability is calculated from a known model. Experimental probability is estimated from observations.
