When the number of each colour is known, we can calculate theoretical probability. When the counters are hidden, we can collect data and estimate probability by relative frequency.

1. Theoretical probability — visible counters

A shallow tray contains 6 red and 4 blue counters. They are scattered, rather than arranged in rows. A counter is selected at random and removed. What is the probability the first counter selected is red?

Before the first draw: P(red) = 6/10 = 0.6

Predict the colour before selecting.

With each counter removed, the composition changes. The displayed 6/10 refers to the first draw only.

2. Experimental probability — hidden counters

Important: Without replacement, probabilities change after each selection. The large bag makes the change small at first, but the draws are not independent. When all 1,000 counters have been drawn and counted, the sample proportion equals the original proportion exactly.

3. Think about it

  1. How does the chance of red change after removing a red counter from the visible tray?
  2. What is the difference between the order in which counters are drawn and the arranged data?
  3. Which is more informative: a sample of 10 or a sample of 100? Why?
  4. Why is counting a sample different from knowing the exact composition of the bag?

Big idea: We calculate theoretical probability from known information. We estimate probability from experiments using relative frequency.
