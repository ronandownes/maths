Counting systematically prevents omissions and double-counting. The most important first question is whether order matters.

1. Multiplication principle

Example

A meal has 3 starters and 4 main courses. How many starter–main combinations are possible?

3 × 4 = 12

Each of the 3 starters can be paired with any of the 4 main courses.

2. When order matters

Four students compete for a first and second prize. There are 4 choices for first and then 3 for second.

4 × 3 = 12 ordered outcomes

3. When order does not matter

Choosing any pair from four students gives half as many outcomes, because AB and BA are the same pair.

(4 × 3) / 2 = 6 unordered pairs
Ask before choosing a formula: Are we arranging distinct positions, or simply selecting a group? Can an item be reused?

4. Practice

  1. A code has one of 5 letters followed by one of 6 digits. Repetition is allowed. How many codes?
  2. There are 5 runners. How many first–second–third finishing orders are possible?
  3. How many unordered pairs can be selected from 5 different people?
Reveal solutions
  1. 5 × 6 = 30.
  2. 5 × 4 × 3 = 60.
  3. 5 × 4 / 2 = 10.
