1. What is being built?

Undefined terms are the starting vocabulary. Definitions introduce mathematical objects using that vocabulary. Axioms are accepted without proof. Theorems are established by valid deductions; corollaries follow from results already established. A converse reverses a statement and must be justified separately.

This is why Geometry is more than identifying shapes. Pupils can first manipulate a picture, then state precisely what the picture represents, and later explain why a claimed property follows from the definitions and axioms.

The official treatment is a school-level presentation of a more formal geometry; its underlying scholarly system is attributed in the document to Patrick D. Barry. Appendix B itself distinguishes what is assumed, what is defined and what is proved.

3. Definitions, in a deliberate sequence

Points, lines and segments

Read the numbered definitions of segment, ray, collinearity, triangle, distance and midpoint. Observe how each new concept depends on earlier language.

Angles and triangles

Study angle types, perpendicularity, congruent triangles, right-angled and isosceles triangles, and parallels.

Polygons and quadrilaterals

Use Definitions 28–34 to distinguish polygon, quadrilateral, rectangle, rhombus, square and parallelogram.

Circles and measurement

Progress from circle terminology to tangent, chords, area and the results that depend on them.

Example: rectangle, rhombus and square

Definition 30: A rectangle is specified by four right angles. Definition 31: A rhombus is specified by four equal sides. Definition 32: A square has both structures: it is a rectangular rhombus.

Consequently, every square is a rectangle and every square is a rhombus. A rectangle need not be a square. The distinction is conceptual, not merely visual.

4. Theorems, converses and corollaries

Develop proofs in logical order rather than treating their conclusions as arbitrary formulae. Examples in the official framework include vertically opposite angles, isosceles triangles, alternate angles, the angle sum of a triangle, corresponding angles, parallelogram properties, Pythagoras and circle geometry.

Students should distinguish a theorem from its converse. Demonstrating a theorem by dragging a diagram can generate a conjecture; it does not, on its own, constitute proof.

7. From exploration to proof

Suggested classroom sequence: see a figure → move or construct it → name its defining properties → test a conjecture → explain → justify rigorously → assess later. The first exploration need not begin with a multiple-choice test.
